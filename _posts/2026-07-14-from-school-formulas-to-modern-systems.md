# From Boredom to Respect

At school, I used to get tired of seeing page after page of mathematics and physics problems.

At that time, I would often think:

> Why am I spending so much time on this?  
> Where is the practical meaning?

To a student, a formula sheet can look like a wall of symbols with no connection to real life. Angles, vectors, derivatives, Laplacians, and field equations can feel far away from anything visible, useful, or human.

What changed for me later was not the formulas themselves. What changed was the **context**.

Once I began working with scientific computing, imaging, machine learning, and quantitative modelling, I realised that many of those old formulas were not just abstract exercises. They were compressed descriptions of how systems behave.

They help us:

- translate assumptions into models,
- turn hypotheses into testable mathematics,
- move from theorem and proof to simulation and implementation,
- reduce complicated nonlinear behaviour into forms we can analyse,
- connect physical structure, probability, learning, and application.

That shift in perspective is what this post is about.

## Why the Unit Circle Matters More Than It First Appears

At school, trigonometry can seem like memorising disconnected formulas:

$$
\sin \theta, \qquad \cos \theta, \qquad \tan \theta
$$

But once the unit circle becomes clear, those formulas stop being isolated rules.

The point

$$
(\cos \theta, \sin \theta)
$$

immediately gives a geometric meaning to angle, position, periodicity, and rotation. That one picture already supports:

- rotations in geometry and robotics,
- periodic signals in physics,
- frequency representations in signal processing,
- coordinate reasoning in image analysis.

This is a good example of a broader pattern in mathematics: one simple structure often reappears in many different forms.

## From Physical Description to Statistical Description

Many classical models begin with deterministic structure:

- scalar functions,
- vector fields,
- gradients,
- divergence,
- curl,
- Laplacian operators.

These ideas help describe motion, flow, diffusion, equilibrium, and propagation.

But in real data, perfect determinism is rare. Measurements are noisy, systems are partial, and observations are incomplete. That is where **probability** enters. Probability does not replace structure; it extends it. It gives us a language for uncertainty, variability, and inference.

This matters because many modern systems are not built from exact equations alone. They sit at the intersection of:

- physical structure,
- statistical uncertainty,
- computational approximation.

## Nonlinearity, Approximation, and Control

One of the most important practical lessons is that complicated nonlinear systems are often analysed through **local structure**.

In many settings, we cannot solve the full system in one perfect step. Instead, we:

1. make assumptions,
2. simplify the model,
3. approximate locally,
4. linearise where useful,
5. analyse stability, sensitivity, or uncertainty,
6. refine the model again.

That is the working logic behind much of applied mathematics.

The same perspective appears in optimization, numerical analysis, control, and machine learning. Even when the final system is nonlinear, a great deal of progress comes from understanding which part can be approximated, which part can be regularised, and which part can be controlled.

This is also where ideas such as **chaotic behaviour**, **entropy**, and **instability** stop being philosophical words and become technical objects that need to be measured, constrained, or modelled.

## Why This Connects to Machine Learning

Machine learning often looks very modern on the surface, but underneath it rests on older mathematical layers:

- **linear algebra** for representations and transformations,
- **calculus** for gradients and optimization,
- **probability** for uncertainty and inference,
- **geometry** for similarity, structure, and embeddings,
- **signal-style thinking** for representation, filtering, and decomposition.

Even deep learning, which is nonlinear overall, is still built from repeated layers of structured linear operations plus nonlinear activations.

In that sense, modern AI did not replace mathematics. It made mathematics even more operational.

## From Deep Learning to ChatGPT-Style Systems

Foundation models and large language models may appear very far from school-level mathematics, but they still depend on the same chain:

- vectors and embeddings,
- matrix multiplications,
- dot products and similarity,
- probability distributions,
- optimization through gradient-based learning.

The surface is conversational. The underlying machinery is mathematical.

This does not reduce these systems to "just formulas." Rather, it shows that the formulas are the mechanism through which the system learns, compresses patterns, and produces responses.

## Why This Matters in Social and Medical Contexts

For me, the most meaningful part is not simply that the mathematics works. It is that the mathematics becomes useful in the world.

These ideas eventually support:

- biomedical imaging,
- disease modelling,
- decision support,
- signal interpretation,
- risk estimation,
- public and social systems analysis.

In medical applications especially, one often moves from theoretical structure to something much more practical:

- how to represent a patient measurement,
- how to reduce noise,
- how to test a biological hypothesis,
- how to interpret a model responsibly,
- how to decide whether a result is robust enough to matter.

So the old formulas that once looked detached from life can become part of a pipeline that affects real people.

## What I Understand Differently Now

When I was younger, I thought mathematics and physics were mainly about solving exercises correctly.

Now I see them more as a hierarchy of understanding:

- notation,
- assumptions,
- structure,
- mechanism,
- proof,
- simulation,
- implementation,
- application.

That is the deeper reason they matter.

Not every formula becomes directly useful every day. But the discipline of thinking through structure, reduction, approximation, and consequence is what later allows theory to become practice.

## Final Reflection

What once looked like wasted effort now looks more like early exposure to the language of systems.

Some of that language appears in a right triangle.  
Some of it appears in a unit circle.  
Some of it appears in gradients, vector fields, and Laplacians.  
Some of it appears in machine learning, deep learning, and foundation models.  
And some of it appears, eventually, in the social and medical problems we care about solving.

That is why I no longer see those old formulas as disconnected school material.

I see them as the beginning of a much larger map.
